Bright Path CoachingThe friend-zone pattern

1The pattern you keep feeling

You know the feeling. Someone comments on your post, DMs you for advice, tells you how much they get from your content, and then a few weeks later you find out they hired a different coach, one who charges more than you and, honestly, maybe knows less than you do.

It doesn't mean your price is too high. It doesn't mean you're bad at sales. It doesn't even mean your marketing is broken, even though marketing is the thing you've probably tried to fix three or four times now. It means your audience learned something from you, over time, one free answer at a time.

They learned that you're the generous one, the one who'll help for free if they just ask nicely. You taught them that, not on purpose, but you taught them. That's not a character flaw. It's a pattern, and patterns can be seen and changed once you know where to look.

What you'll walk away with

  • The exact moment the free-help pattern starts, named in your own recent activity.
  • Why raising your price alone never fixes it.
  • The first concrete move out of the friend zone.

2Name it in your own numbers

Write by hand if you can. The point is to make the pattern concrete, not abstract.

1Think back over the last 90 days. Write down three moments where someone got real, substantial help from you for free, whether in a comment, a DM, or a live.
What this reveals: the free-help pattern in your own recent activity.
2Look at your last five discovery calls. How many did you discount, and what did you tell yourself in the moment that made the discount feel necessary?
What this reveals: the guilt-driven pricing pattern, straight from your own calls.
3Finish this sentence honestly: "If I raised my price and someone asked for a discount, I would probably..." Write what you'd actually do, not what you think you should do.
What this reveals: the gap between your stated price and your behavior under pressure.

Where you are now

You post daily, go live, run the challenges, book the calls. And still, the warmest people, the ones who tell you how much you've helped them, go hire someone else.
